    Which methods do I use in systemic consulting and coaching for universities?

    Last updated: November 2026

    S03 · Consulting methods — H2 · intro | Image ("Picture option") · 10 rows (number · H3 | text with links), CSS ID per row = doc anchor

    Methods of systemic consulting and awareness-based systems change

    In my work with universities, I use methods that combine analytical systems thinking with a view of the organisation as a whole. The following 10 methods are examples of those I use in my work.

    01

    What is Otto Scharmer’s Theory U, and how does it help with deep change?

    Otto Scharmer, Senior Lecturer at MIT and co-founder of the Presencing Institute, first published Theory U in 2007 in his book “Theory U: Leading from the Future as It Emerges”. The model describes how attention, intention and the capacity to act shift when a person or group lets go of old knowledge, really listens and develops something new from the future as it emerges. The U-shaped arc that gives the model its name leads down the left-hand side through letting go of old judgements. At the bottom comes presencing: a moment of stillness in which a new direction becomes clear. And the right-hand side leads back into action, into prototypes that connect head, heart and hand. In organisational development for universities, Theory U provides the framework in which methods such as Case Clinic and 3D and 4D mapping find their place.

    02

    What does agility mean, and how can agile ways of working be used at universities?

    Agility describes an organisation’s ability to respond quickly and purposefully to change. It has its origins in the Agile Manifesto, written in 2001 by 17 people from software development. Among other things, it values collaboration with those involved over rigid agreements, and responding to change over following a plan. Working methods such as Scrum and Kanban grew out of it. What they have in common: work is divided into short stages, made visible and reviewed together at regular intervals. Universities don’t have to become software teams for this. Agile principles help break projects into smaller steps, distribute responsibility clearly within teams and negotiate priorities openly, for example with a shared Kanban board for a strategy project or short work cycles in a working group. I use agile ways of working especially where universities want to get moving faster without giving up participation and careful decision-making.

    03

    What is design thinking, and how does it help develop services from the perspective of the people they are meant for?

    Design thinking emerged in the 1990s around the design agency IDEO and was developed further from 2005 at the d.school at Stanford University and from 2007 at the Hasso Plattner Institute in Potsdam. The approach puts the needs of the people a solution is meant for at the centre. In several steps, an interdisciplinary team moves from understanding and observing, through defining the real question, to ideas, prototypes and tests. A frequently used tool is the persona: a concrete profile of a typical user, based on conversations and data. In work with universities, design thinking helps to rethink study programmes, exchange formats or services from the perspective of students, teaching staff and other staff. With the leadership of Bifröst University, for example, I developed personas for outgoing and incoming exchange students. I use design thinking especially in the “Notice” and “Align” steps, when a university wants to redesign a service and needs to understand what the people who use it really need.

    04

    What is 3D mapping, and how does it help make system dynamics visible?

    3D mapping is a method from the toolbox of the Presencing Institute in which participants use tangible materials, objects or symbols to build a model of the current situation and how they would like it to develop. Unlike the physical, theatre-based 4D mapping, 3D mapping works with objects rather than movement, which means it can also be used online. In organisational development for universities, it helps make hidden relationships and tensions within an organisation visible that are often hard to grasp through analysis alone. I use 3D mapping especially to make a system visible to everyone involved and open up new perspectives on it.

    05

    What is Art of Hosting, and how does participation work in large groups?

    Art of Hosting, in full “The Art of Hosting and Harvesting Conversations that Matter”, is a practice for conversations that make a difference. It emerged in the late 1990s and has since been developed further by an international, self-organising community of process facilitators. Art of Hosting brings together several formats, including World Café, Open Space and Appreciative Inquiry, and chooses among them to suit the purpose of a meeting. Harvesting is an integral part: results are captured in a way that keeps them working after the meeting. In organisational development for universities, Art of Hosting makes it possible to include many perspectives without processes getting out of hand, for example in strategy processes with teaching staff, students and administration, or at meetings of a university alliance. I use Art of Hosting especially when a university wants to encourage participation and needs formats in which even large groups can think together and reach results.

    06

    What is Baldwin and Linnea’s The Circle Way, and how can it be used to structure group conversations?

    Christina Baldwin and Ann Linnea developed The Circle Way over a period of around 25 years from circle practices in various cultures, and in 2010 they published the book of the same name, “The Circle Way: A Leader in Every Chair”. The method structures conversations through seating in a circle, a jointly held purpose and clear social agreements in which every voice carries equal weight. In leadership bodies and alliance structures, the seating arrangement alone changes how a conversation unfolds. I work with The Circle Way especially in organisational development for universities, in leadership teams or university alliances where, until now, mostly the loudest voices have been heard.

    07

    What is consent decision-making, and how does it help committees make good decisions faster?

    The consent principle comes from sociocracy, developed in the 1970s by the Dutch entrepreneur Gerard Endenburg in his own company, building on ideas from the educator Kees Boeke. Under consent, a decision counts as made when nobody has a serious, reasoned objection. Unlike consensus, not everyone has to agree. The question is whether a proposal is good enough to try and safe enough to implement. Objections count as valuable information that improves the proposal. For universities, consent decision-making answers a central question: how decisions can be made faster without anyone being overruled. I use consent decision-making especially in leadership teams, working groups and alliance bodies when decisions get stuck in long coordination loops or are postponed again and again.

    08

    What are prototyping and retrospectives, and how do they turn a strategy into practice?

    Prototyping forms the right-hand side of the arc in Theory U: instead of working out a plan in full before anything happens, first ideas are tested early in small, concrete experiments and developed further with feedback from those involved. Retrospectives come from agile practice. At fixed intervals, a team looks back together: what worked, what didn’t, and what do we change next? In organisational development for universities, the two approaches combine into a rhythm of trying out, evaluating and adjusting. This makes it possible, for example, to test new teaching formats with AI or new forms of collaboration in an alliance before they are introduced across the university. I use prototyping and retrospectives especially in the “Act with impact” step, so that strategies don’t stop at the concept stage but last in day-to-day work at the university.

    09

    What is Spiral Dynamics, and how does the model help in understanding organisational culture?

    The developmental psychologist Clare W. Graves laid the theoretical foundation in the late 1960s and early 1970s. Don Beck and Christopher Cowan worked with him from 1975 and published their further development in 1996 under the title “Spiral Dynamics: Mastering Values, Leadership, and Change”. The model describes how the value systems of individuals, groups and organisations develop in response to their living conditions, not as a ranking but as a development that fits the respective context. In organisational development for universities, the model helps to understand why different faculties or administrative units react differently to the same push for change. I draw on Spiral Dynamics especially to make sense of recurring conflicts between different cultures within a university, for example between established faculties and newly emerging institutes.

    10

    What is a teal organisation according to Frédéric Laloux, and what does it mean for universities?

    Frédéric Laloux coined the term in 2014 in his book “Reinventing Organizations”. He describes organisations characterised by self-management and distributed responsibility instead of rigid hierarchy, by wholeness in how they treat their members, and by a deep, shared sense of their purpose in society. For universities, this doesn’t mean copying a particular organisational form wholesale, but a direction: more participation in decisions, faster adaptation to new demands, less friction from rigid responsibilities. I use the concept of the teal organisation especially in organisational development for universities, when a university is thinking about its future leadership and organisational structure.

    Methods of systemic coaching

    In one-to-one support, in coaching leadership teams and in leadership development, I work with tools from systemic coaching. They help clarify your own position in the system and sort out inner contradictions. And they help find the one that works among many possible solutions. The following six tools come from different traditions of systemic practice and can be used individually or in combination.

    11

    What is Stephen Covey’s Circle of Influence, and how does it help in leadership roles?

    Stephen Covey developed the Circle of Influence and published it in 1989 in his book “The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People”. The model distinguishes two areas: what a person can actually influence (Circle of Influence), and what concerns them but isn’t directly in their hands (Circle of Concern). In coaching, the distinction helps put energy where impact is possible, especially in committees and governance structures where many decisions lie outside your own control. I use the Circle of Influence especially at the start of coaching, when a situation first seems overwhelming. The first step is then to separate real power to act from mere worries.

    12

    What is Steve de Shazer’s miracle question, and how does it help in coaching?

    Steve de Shazer and Insoo Kim Berg developed the miracle question in the 1980s at the Brief Family Therapy Center in Milwaukee, as part of solution-focused brief therapy. It invites you to imagine a future in which the current problem has already been solved, and to derive concrete first steps from it. In coaching university leaders, it shifts attention away from the problem and makes visible the resources that are often overlooked in day-to-day work. I use the miracle question especially when a conversation keeps circling around a problem and the goal is still blurred.

    13

    What is Schulz von Thun’s Inner Team, and how does it help with decisions?

    Friedemann Schulz von Thun introduced the model of the Inner Team in 1998 in “Miteinander reden: Das Innere Team und situationsgerechte Kommunikation”. It describes how every person carries several inner voices with different concerns, such as a cautious one and a brave one. In coaching university leaders, the model makes these voices visible and helps organise them into a team that can act, instead of letting them work against each other. This is especially valuable for decisions with a lot at stake personally. I work with the Inner Team especially before important decisions, when several seemingly contradictory voices within one person are competing to be heard.

    14

    What is Claes Janssen’s Four Rooms of Change, and how does it help in change processes?

    The Swedish psychologist Claes Janssen developed the model on the basis of his 1975 doctoral thesis “Personal Dialectics” at Stockholm University. It describes four psychological rooms that people move through in change processes: contentment, denial, confusion and renewal. In coaching leaders and leadership teams, the model helps to recognise which room a person is in right now and which movement comes next. Confusion or denial are not a step backwards. I draw on the Four Rooms of Change especially in transition phases, to place a currently confusing or frustrating situation within a familiar process that people can move through.

    15

    What is Social Presencing Theater, and how does it help in coaching?

    Social Presencing Theater is a body-based practice that Arawana Hayashi developed together with Otto Scharmer at the Presencing Institute. The exercises work with the body rather than with words, for example in Stuck. A person or group moves from a first, intuitively taken posture representing the current situation (Sculpture 1) to a second posture that suggests a possible development (Sculpture 2). In coaching and in work with leadership teams, Social Presencing Theater comes in where words reach their limits. It makes stuck patterns accessible through body awareness. I use these exercises especially when a topic can no longer be moved forward through language and a physical approach opens up new movement.

    16

    What is a Case Clinic, and how does it help in peer community work?

    The Case Clinic is a structured peer consultation format from the toolbox of the Presencing Institute. One person brings a current question. A small group of three to four colleagues first listens in silence and then shares the images, feelings and gestures they noticed. Afterwards, we move into an open dialogue to open up new perspectives. In leadership programmes, leadership teams and peer groups, for example in my offer for women in university leadership, this format helps the group benefit from its collective capacity to perceive, rather than just receiving individual pieces of advice. I use the Case Clinic especially to make a new kind of listening and mutual support among colleagues possible.

    FAQ

    Which methods does U-teal use in systemic consulting and coaching for universities?

    I work with 16 proven methods from strategy consulting, organisational development and coaching: Theory U, 3D mapping, Art of Hosting, The Circle Way, consent decision-making, agile ways of working, design thinking, prototyping and retrospectives, teal organisations, Spiral Dynamics, Circle of Influence, the miracle question, the Inner Team, Four Rooms of Change, Social Presencing Theater and Case Clinics.

    When is Theory U relevant as a method for universities?

    In deep change processes, where old knowledge is let go and something new is allowed to emerge from the future as it unfolds.

    How can universities become more agile?

    Agile ways of working help universities break projects into manageable steps and see first results early. They make work and priorities visible to everyone involved, strengthen responsibility within teams and create fixed moments to check together what is working. This lets universities respond faster to new demands while involving everyone who carries out the change.

    What is design thinking used for at universities?

    To design study programmes, exchange formats or services from the perspective of the people who use them. Personas and early prototypes make visible what students, teaching staff and other staff really need.

    What is 3D mapping used for in organisational development?

    To make hidden relationships and tensions within an organisation visible that are often hard to grasp through analysis alone. It can also be used online.

    When is Art of Hosting suitable for universities?

    When many perspectives are meant to feed into a strategy or change process, for example from teaching staff, students and administration, or from the partner universities of an alliance, and the results are meant to keep working afterwards.

    When does working with The Circle Way help in institutional processes?

    Especially in leadership teams or university alliances where, until now, mostly the loudest voices have set the tone: seating in a circle gives every voice equal weight.

    How does consent decision-making help university committees?

    It enables faster decisions without giving up participation: a proposal counts as accepted when nobody has a serious, reasoned objection. Objections improve the proposal instead of blocking it.

    How do prototyping and retrospectives help implement strategies at universities?

    They create a rhythm of trying out, evaluating and adjusting. New approaches are tested on a small scale before they are introduced across the university.

    What does the concept of the teal organisation mean in practice for a university?

    Not copying a particular organisational form wholesale, but a direction: more participation in decisions, faster adaptation, less friction from rigid responsibilities.

    What is Spiral Dynamics used for in university development?

    To make sense of recurring conflicts between different cultures within a university, for example between established faculties and newly emerging institutes.

    When is the Circle of Influence particularly helpful?

    Especially at the start of a coaching process, when a situation seems overwhelming and the task is to separate your own power to act from mere worries.

    What is the miracle question used for in coaching?

    To shift attention away from the problem and make visible resources that are often overlooked in day-to-day work.

    Why is the Inner Team model important for decisions?

    Because it makes contradictory inner voices visible before important decisions. And it helps organise them into a team that can act, instead of letting them work against each other.

    When does the Four Rooms of Change model help in change processes?

    Especially in transition phases, to place a confusing or frustrating situation within a familiar process that people can move through.

    What is Social Presencing Theater used for in coaching?

    Where words reach their limits: the body-based exercises, such as Stuck, make stuck patterns accessible through body awareness.

    When is the Case Clinic used in peer communities?

    When one person brings a current question and the group is meant to benefit from its collective capacity to perceive, rather than just giving individual pieces of advice.
